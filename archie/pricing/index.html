Modelled from measured component sizes and the AI companies’ published prices in July 2026, for one agent on Claude Sonnet at the Balanced response quality, over a 30-day month.

Per-message times thirty won’t reach the monthly total, and that isn’t a typo. The rate counts only the messages you send; the total also covers what your agent does on its own schedule, which is the split beside each one. An agent gets expensive through steady use rather than through any one costly question, and worked hard it can pass most software subscriptions. The third driver below is the dial for that.

It’s the passes that add up

Your agent has no memory of its own, so every reply starts by handing it a fresh briefing: everything it needs to know about you and the job, plus the conversation so far. A question it can answer from that briefing alone takes one pass. One that makes it look something up, read the answer, then look up something else takes three or four, each re-sending the whole briefing. Passes multiply the bill, not the length of your question.

Cost follows what you connect

Every service has to be described in that briefing before your agent can use it. A calendar-only agent carries a short one. Wire in an inbox, a to-do list, its own lists and a few other services, and the briefing runs about four and a half times longer, so the identical question costs roughly 2.8 times as much. Worth knowing before you connect everything you own.

Response quality is the dial you hold

Economy, Balanced and Best cost very different amounts per message, and every figure above is Balanced, the default. Running that heavy agent entirely on Economy takes roughly two thirds off it. That trades against how good the answers are, which is why it’s your switch and not our default.

Archie takes the cheap route when there is one

The AI companies let you park a copy of the briefing on their side, so the next pass points at it instead of sending it again. Reading that copy costs about a tenth of the usual price, but making it costs about a quarter more, so it’s a bet rather than a saving. Archie makes that bet one reply at a time, on every agent, with nothing to set up.

Measured

The size of the briefing, the AI companies’ published prices, and how many passes an ordinary message takes. Counted from the real thing, several of them against agents that have been running for weeks.

Estimated

How much the model works a problem through before answering. That working bills at five times the rate of reading and varies with the question, so it’s the widest unknown here: a heavy agent could land anywhere from about $118 to about $301 a month. The table uses the middle.

Assumed

How people space their messages out across a day, which is what the copy-keeping decision turns on. Reasonable patterns rather than measured ones.

None of this asks you to take our word for it. Archie keeps its own running total on the Account screen, this month and all time, by AI company and by agent. It’s an estimate from a price table inside the app, not the AI company’s invoice, and it says so on screen. The real bill is on the AI company’s dashboard, where you can also set a hard spending cap.

What It’s Worth

Put It Next to the Alternative

The job you’re handing your agent (the inbox, the calendar, the follow-ups, the research, the small things that eat a week) is the job you’d otherwise hand a person.

A part-time personal assistant can run thousands of dollars a year. They also go home at five, take holidays, and have to be told the same thing twice.

Your agent doesn’t. It’s there at six in the morning when you want the day laid out, and at eleven at night when you remember the thing you forgot, for as long as your computer is on and Archie is running.

That’s the trade: tens of dollars a month rather than thousands a year, for help that never clocks out.
